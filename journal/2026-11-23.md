# 2026-11-23: Stop, look back, start over

**Stage:** 0 finished, stage 1 started

## What happened

My forward test had been losing for weeks, and I could not explain why. When I traced it back, I
found that the version I was forward-testing had **never been backtested**. It came from my own
discretionary feel for what worked. I also realized that most of the system had been built
without me checking each step. So today I stopped adding features and restarted the research from
the beginning.

## What I did

1. **Dropped the untested variant.** I went back to the version that has a backtest behind it.
   A forward test should confirm a backtest. It is too slow to discover an edge.
2. **Chose the stricter definition of the pattern.** It makes less money in the historical test,
   but the looser one assumed fills that were not realistic: price had already moved past the
   entry level, and the backtest still filled at that level. I would rather test the correct
   definition than the profitable one.
3. **Compared two data vendors at zero cost.** I checked one vendor's historical minute bars
   against my broker's minute bars.
   - On index ETFs (619 days), 93% of bars had identical highs and lows.
   - On single stocks (45 days), 83% were identical, and almost all the rest differed by a cent
     or less.
   - Only about 0.1% of bars differed by more than 5 cents.

   The data was fine. The problems were in my own code.
4. **Found two serious biases in my own universe code:**
   - **Survivorship bias.** Company details were looked up without a date, so stocks that were
     later delisted came back empty and were silently dropped. Almost every stock in five years of
     history was still listed today, which cannot be right.
   - **Look-ahead bias.** Stocks were screened with that day's *closing* price and full-day volume.
     At the open, when the real decision is made, neither number exists yet.
5. **Wrote one page that lists every rule.** For each rule: where it came from (learned from
   discretionary traders, or added by me), why it exists, and whether the backtest and the live
   system actually do the same thing. Several rules failed that check.
6. **Removed three rules that had no basis.** Two came from nowhere. The third was contradicted
   by my own data.

## What I learned

- **The backtest and live trading must test the same thing.** I was choosing stocks by hand
  live, while the backtest chose them with a mechanical rule. The two were not measuring the same
  strategy, so neither one could validate the other.
- **The garden of forking paths.** I had picked a profit target because it was the most
  profitable on past data. Then I removed a time limit because that target took too long to reach.
  Each step looked reasonable, but the second rule rested on a first rule that was never properly
  tested. Errors stack.
- **Picking the best value on the same data you report on is in-sample optimization.** If you try
  20 values and keep the best, part of its performance is luck. The fix is to choose on a
  development set and confirm once on data you never touched.
- **Pre-registration.** For the two rules that had been chosen this way, I wrote down a short
  list of candidates *before* looking at any new result. They will be compared once, and I will
  accept the outcome without adding new candidates afterwards.
- **A rule can be right for the wrong reason.** "Hold until the stop, the target or the close" is
  the standard exit for an intraday strategy. My reason for adopting it was wrong, but the rule
  itself stands on its own.
- **Data cleaning is not stock selection.** Dropping "bad days" with too many empty minutes
  looked like cleaning, but it was secretly filtering out illiquid stocks. Live trading had no such
  filter. Cleaning should only remove corrupt data. Liquidity belongs in the selection rules, using
  information known before the open.
- **Adjusted prices are wrong for this kind of backtest.** Split adjustment rewrites history. One
  stock that did several reverse splits showed an adjusted price in the hundreds of millions of
  dollars, against an actual price of about $1.40. Price filters and per-share costs have to use
  the prices that actually traded.

## Decisions

- Data: one vendor for history (it matches the broker closely), five years to start, and missing
  no-trade minutes are filled the way the broker shows them, so that patterns are counted on the
  same bars live and in the backtest.
- The profit target and the trading-hours rule are pre-registered as small candidate sets, to be
  decided at stage 5.
